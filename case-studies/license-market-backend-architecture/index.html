LicenseMarket: faster APIs, dependable operations, and a backend team

At LicenseMarket, I work on a commerce and CRM platform used by customers, operators, and accounting colleagues. My role combines implementation and production support with managing a backend team of eight. The work spans the everyday paths that make an operating business function: orders, tickets, wallet transactions, refunds, reporting, and communication.

Result and scope

40% lower average API latency and 99.9% availability over approximately six months; management of eight backend engineers.

A platform with several kinds of pressure

An operator looking for a customer needs a quick answer. Accounting needs an explanation for a refund or balance change. A customer submitting an order needs a consistent result, even when another service is slow. Those requirements meet in the same backend, so a local improvement has to preserve the surrounding business rules.

My work includes NestJS services, PostgreSQL access through Prisma, Redis caching, and RabbitMQ background work. I also develop ticketing, document automation, and AI-assisted chat workflows. The breadth matters because a slow screen can be caused by its query, its response shape, repeated work in a service, or an integration waiting on something else.

Database performance starts with the request

The performance work focused on database and API response time. I tightened read paths, bounded queries, worked on indexes, and used caching where repeated reads justified it. The aim was to reduce unnecessary work while keeping the response useful to the application consuming it.

There is a practical distinction between making a database operation cheaper and making an API faster. A smaller query may still sit inside a loop; a fast cached response may become incorrect if its invalidation rules are incomplete. My review process therefore follows the data from the screen through the service and back, including filters, relationships, and serialization.

Over approximately six months, our database/API timing tests showed a 40% reduction in average API latency. That is the confirmed scope of the result. It is not a percentile claim, a benchmark for every endpoint, or a promise that an index alone produced the entire improvement.

Financial workflows need an explanation trail

I worked with accounting colleagues on refunds, wallet transactions, and reports. These features are connected: a balance change has to make sense in the operational screen and in the report used later to reconcile it. An implementation that updates a number without preserving the meaning of the operation is difficult to support.

The useful engineering questions are concrete. Which action changed the balance? Which actor initiated it? Is the report filtering the intended records? Does an old record still produce a usable response after the API adds a field? These are the kinds of boundaries I work through with the people who use the system, rather than treating reporting as an afterthought.

Production work includes recovery and observability

My responsibilities also include Kubernetes, Grafana monitoring, database administration, and Bash/PowerShell automation. Availability is tracked through UptimeRobot, our monitoring system, and an incident panel in the internal CRM. The confirmed availability figure is 99.9% over approximately six months.

I treat an availability percentage as the start of an operational conversation. It needs a service boundary and a period, and it needs incident records to explain interruptions. This page describes the confirmed result without exposing internal infrastructure, customer records, or active operational procedures.

Related work includes VoIP server work and a Mattermost plugin addressing message/channel access, search behavior, and responsiveness. Those are separate engineering concerns; hashing by itself is not a claim of message encryption or end-to-end confidentiality.

Leading the team while remaining hands-on

I began managing backend engineers in early 2024. My responsibilities include direct reports, hiring, one-to-ones, performance reviews, mentoring, and delivery planning. I use GitHub Projects and issues to organize delivery, with pull-request review and CI/CD supporting the implementation flow.

Mentoring happens through actual project work: working through a change, discussing the data model or service boundary, and reviewing the result in context. That keeps technical feedback connected to decisions an engineer has to make on the next task.

The role includes coordinating more than five major releases. I have not attached invented velocity, hiring, or retention percentages to that work. The stronger evidence is the combination of confirmed management scope and continued responsibility for code, database behavior, and production support.

What this work demonstrates

The central challenge is balancing delivery with the cost of operating the system after release. Query performance, financial correctness, and understandable incidents all affect whether a team can ship the next change with confidence.

This case study is a high-level account of shared production work. It describes my contribution and the outcomes I can support; it does not claim that I built the entire platform alone.

Evidence and boundaries

Metrics are owner-confirmed, based on private timing tests, UptimeRobot, monitoring, and CRM incident records. Raw telemetry and company source are not public. Recent internal incident details are intentionally excluded.
